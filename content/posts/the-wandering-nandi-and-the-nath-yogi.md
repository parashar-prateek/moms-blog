In the past, Indian towns and villages often saw a beautiful spiritual sight. A Sadhu, with sacred ash on his forehead, would walk down the street leading a decorated live bull. This majestic animal represented Nandi, the sacred vehicle of Lord Shiva. These holy men belonged to the ancient Nath tradition, started by Guru Matsyendranath and Guru Gorakhnath. This custom brought a divine presence right to people's doorsteps.

Years ago, the musical sound of heavy brass bells would fill the air. The moment the bells rang in the street, neighborhood children would run out of their houses with pure excitement.

When the bull and the Sadhu finally stopped in front of a home, everyone stood frozen in wonder. The Nandi wore a colorful embroidered dushala covered in shiny white cowrie shells that clinked softly as he moved. It felt as though a living temple had come to bless the doorstep, filling the whole area with purity and happiness.

The Sadhu would loudly chant, "Alakh Niranjan!" This is the sacred greeting of the Nath tradition. In simple terms, Alakh means God who is invisible and formless, while Niranjan means something completely pure and untouched by impurity. By chanting these words, the Yogi was gently reminding everyone that God is purely present inside everything and everyone around them. Families would warmly repeat the same words back to him.

These Sadhus were deeply respected because they were not greedy. They never asked for money or expensive things. Elders would scoop fresh atta (wheat flour) and whole grains into the Sadhu's cloth bag (jhola), which he accepted happily with a genuine smile.

Adults treated the bull as a holy shrine. They would bow down, touch the animal's feet for blessings, and feed it fresh grain as Nandi gently nodded his decorated head. People believed that feeding Nandi cleared away bad karma and brought good luck. In return, the Sadhu blessed the home with peace and happiness, creating a lovely cycle of giving and gratitude.

---

Today, however, the world looks completely different. Over time, people's mindset has changed from trust to deep doubt. In the past, people automatically trusted holy men, but today, modern skepticism makes people quickly question whether a wandering Sadhu is real or fake. Because of this suspicion, people often close their hearts and doors, forgetting that true monks still exist and depend on simple offerings of grain to live. At the same time, homes with open doors have been replaced by high-rise apartments, security guards, and high walls that block out these wandering traditions.

Also, quiet streets have turned into busy, noisy roads, making city lanes unsafe for peaceful walking. Even though this tradition has faded from concrete cities, it is still very much alive in the villages of Maharashtra, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh. It can also be seen during big spiritual festivals like the Kumbh Mela. In those quiet village lanes, away from traffic and high walls, the decorated Nandi still walks safely.

Farmers still offer grain with pure devotion, and open doors still welcome the timeless echo of "Alakh Niranjan!"

---

At its heart, this tradition was much more than just a way for Sadhus to ask for Bhiksha. It was a living reminder of faith, community bonding, and childhood joy. When old traditions fade quietly, we do not just lose a custom - we lose a piece of our shared soul. While it is gone from modern cities, it remains a special part of India's heritage, reminding us of a simpler time when the divine wandered from one mohalla to the next, and people gladly ran out to welcome it.

Though our customs, culture, and traditions have faded in their old forms, they have not vanished or disappeared.

Every generation tries to connect with and contribute to our heritage in its own way. Even in today's fast-paced, noisy world - where phones and gadgets often create silent walls between family members sitting in the same room - our traditions remain a powerful bridge that pulls us away from our screens and reconnects us with our roots and with one another.

We see this revival among Gen Z and young adults through "Bhajan Clubbing" and "Bhajan Jamming," where traditional devotional songs fill cafes and concert halls with fresh energy. Similarly, the massive box-office success of films like Hanuman Ansh and Ramayana shows how deeply people still crave connection with our faith and values. The impact of recent festivals like Dussehra and Diwali, along with films like Ramayana, can be seen in local parks and marketplaces today, where small children happily carry tiny dhanush (bows) in their hands.

Seeing that innocent curiosity gives us hope for the future.

---

While modern life may change how traditions look, we each hold a simple responsibility: whatever little we know about our customs, rituals, and heritage, we must pass it on to our children, our grandchildren, and anyone interested in knowing about them. By sharing these stories, we keep the spirit of our culture - and the timeless memory of the wandering Nandi and the Nath Yogi - alive for generations to come!
